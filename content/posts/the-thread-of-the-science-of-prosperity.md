The second book of the _The Nitopadesha_, _The Thread of the Science of Prosperity_ is a textbook of economics. It goes in to the intricacies of the market and talks about supply and demand, prices, opportunity costs, and even managing common goods.

As I had mentioned in the first part of this review/exploration of _The Nitopadesha_ it feels surreal to learn how wise and intelligent our ancestors were and the same time saddening to see how contemporary Indian politics and policymaking being stuck in wrong economics assumptions.

Perhaps our politicians and policymakers would be better at their jobs if they read _The Nitopadesha_ or if they at least took a minute to listen to what Bellikota, the cat had to say:

{{< blockquote title="The Story of the Generous Bear, The Nitopadesha" >}}
A price is right, just and fair\
Only when it reflects the scarcity of the resource,\
And truly counts all costs incurred\
And the opportunities foregone.
{{< /blockquote >}}

Without further ado, let's explore the lessons in this book. Unlike part 1, where I went in a bit more detail into the story in each chapter, I will focus only on the key takeaways. I would once again encourage everyone to read _The Nitopadesha_ and use these articles as a secondary tool to aid your reading and refer in the future.

The second book follows Udaradevan and Bellikota as they exchange their stories.

## The Price is Right

Story name: _The Story of the Generous Bear_
About a generous bear named Udaradevan.

Effort adds value and this is reflected in the price.

Bad pricing makes either the consumers or the sellers greedy.

Good pricing should reflect the scarcity of a resource.

Interesting story as it mentions things like supply-demand curve and opportunity costs.

Udaradevan goes to the next story about his gradfather.

## What Do You Do Best?

Story name: _The Good Bandit_
About Udaradevan's grandfather Tarakaran.

Differences create exchanges.

Differences are natural.

In a trade, both the seller and the buyer walk away happy because both value different things.

But it also means everyone should focus on what they do best and exchange it for what they value more.

This is the problem I have with self-sufficiency policies like "Aatmanirbhar Bharat." Autonomy makes sense when it is strategic, like military or communications technology. But there isn't any reason for us to try to be self sufficient in everything. While such policies do sound good, it distorts the free market and ultimately hurts the consumers.

In the next story Bellikota narrates about his three second cousins once removed we learn how differences cause us to be motivated by different things.

## Incentives Matter (Working title, looking for alternatives)

Story name: _Story of the Singing Cats_
About Bellikota's cousins, Rakamma, Thanakka, and Pallavi.

{{< blockquote title="Story of the Singing Cats, The Nitopadesha" >}}
For without incentives no one moves, speeds up or changes direction.
{{< /blockquote >}}

This third story is set in Kovai and its characters have very Tamilian names which is pretty interesting. I wonder how big these civilizations were to have this breadth of knowledge. Truly we have lost a lot.

People act with incentives and don't act without incentives.

Different incetives work different people in different situations.

Different types of incentives: Higher status/respect, monetary incentives, force/violence/backmail.

For example traffic violation should have a monetary incentive to not pay the fine. Other incentives would not work.

Bellikota's story reminds Udaradevan about the Fearsome Four-Eyed Dog and he moves on to the next story.

## Markets are Fairer than you think (Working title, could be improved)

Story name: _The Handiwork of the Unseen Spirit_
About Udaradevan's grandfather Tarakaran again and the counsel he got from the Fearsome Four-Eyed Dog from first book perhaps.

{{< blockquote title="The Handiwork of the Unseen Spirit, The Nitopadesha" >}}
Yet know this, bear! What we call the market is fairer than others that you can imagine.
{{< /blockquote >}}

Markets are fair. It ensures that society produces right quantities of the right things at the right price. Markets aligns self-interest with that of others.

What we often confuse as a failure of markets or cApItailSm is just crony-capitalism, where a business gains an unfair advantage through close ties with the government. Think about all the times you were angry at company; did the company have close ties with the government?

When Udaradevan finished his story Bellikota remarks that "there are important conditions for markets to work, and they must be well understood. There are important conditions when markets fail, and they too must be understood."

Bellikota drives this point home by narrating the story of his first cousin twice removed in the next story.
